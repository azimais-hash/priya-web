<p class="eyebrow">The quiet exit</p>

## The women who step back rarely say so in advance

By their late forties and fifties, many of the most experienced women in an organisation are at a decision point. A role that has stopped fitting. A promotion that no longer appeals. Menopause arriving in the middle of a demanding job. Ageing parents, teenagers, a restructure, a long-held plan that needs rethinking.

Most of that thinking happens privately. The organisation usually hears about it late, in a resignation, a withdrawal from progression, or a quiet decision to stop putting herself forward.

This work gives those women somewhere rigorous and confidential to think while the decision is still open, and gives you a reason for them to stay.

<p class="eyebrow">Why it matters now</p>

<div class="specialisms">
<div>
<h3>Menopause reaches the work itself</h3>
<p>CIPD's 2023 study of more than 2,000 UK working women aged 40 to 60 found that 67% of those experiencing menopause symptoms said the symptoms had a mostly negative effect at work, and 27% reported a negative impact on career progression.</p>
</div>
<div>
<h3>Most employers have the intent, few have the provision</h3>
<p>In the DWP's 2024 employer survey, 64% of employers agreed that workplaces should provide support relating to menopause, while 18% had a menopause policy and 5% reported menopause training.</p>
</div>
<div>
<h3>Caring lands on the same decade</h3>
<p>The ONS estimates 1.4 million sandwich carers in the UK, 61% of them women, and 51% aged 45 to 64. Many are carrying senior responsibility at the same time.</p>
</div>
<div>
<h3>Careers now run far longer</h3>
<p>Women aged 50 to 64 had a 68.2% employment rate in 2025, the highest in the recent series, and women leave the labour market at an average age of 64.7.</p>
</div>
</div>

<p style="margin-top:2.5rem">From April 2026, employers with 250 or more employees have been encouraged to publish voluntary action plans alongside gender pay gap reporting, with menopause-related support among the areas covered by government guidance.</p>
</div>
</section>

## How your leaders show up when the pressure is on

The Energy Leadership Index is a research-backed attitudinal assessment, recommended by Forbes and available only through iPEC-certified coaches. It looks at attitudes and perceptions, and shows how they shift between an ordinary day and a stressful one.

Every day, mindset shapes how people lead, collaborate and decide. The ELI makes those patterns visible, so a leader can see what she does under pressure and choose differently.

<div class="actions"><a class="btn-link" href="https://www.ipeccoaching.com/blog/energy-leadership-index">Read more about the ELI</a></div>
</div>

### Why energy matters

<ul class="ticks">
<li><strong>Beyond personality tests.</strong> Unlike static personality assessments, the ELI looks at attitudes and perceptions and shows how they change in different situations.</li>
<li><strong>Seven levels of leadership.</strong> You see where a leader spends most of her energy, from stressful, draining states to those that inspire creativity and performance.</li>
<li><strong>Real business impact.</strong> Leaders who draw on constructive ("anabolic") energy see better team engagement, greater innovation and stronger results.</li>
<li><strong>Quick online assessment.</strong> About 20 minutes answering questions on how she thinks and reacts in everyday situations.</li>
<li><strong>Personal debrief.</strong> A 60 to 90 minute session with me, a certified Energy Leadership practitioner, to explore the results and identify where the growth sits.</li>
<li><strong>Action plan.</strong> Together we map the shifts that would make the most difference to how she leads.</li>
</ul>

### Three ways organisations use it

<ul class="ticks">
<li><strong>Individual insight.</strong> For executives and managers who want to raise self-awareness and sharpen their impact.</li>
<li><strong>Team experience.</strong> Gives a whole team shared language for communication, accountability and trust.</li>
<li><strong>360 degree perspective.</strong> Adds feedback from colleagues and stakeholders for a fuller picture of a leader's energy.</li>
</ul>

<p class="eyebrow">Maternity returners</p>
<h2>The return to work is a transition like any other</h2>
<p>Coming back after maternity leave is a pivotal moment for the woman and for the organisation. Without support, capable women can lose confidence, question their place, and start planning an exit long before they announce one.</p>
<p>I work with returners before and after the return: what she wants this phase of her career to look like, how she holds her professional identity alongside a new one, and how she handles the first demanding months with some steadiness.</p>
<p>Nine sessions is my usual recommendation, four before she returns and four or five afterwards.</p>
