The lights are going up on the high street, the adverts have started, and everyone around you seems to be making plans and talking about what they are looking forward to. And yet, for you, something feels very different this year. Perhaps even the thought of Christmas feels heavy. If that is where you are right now, I want you to know that you are far from alone, and that there is nothing wrong with you for feeling this way.

## Christmas is not always the most wonderful time of the year

For many people, this season brings up feelings that are hard to explain to anyone else. You might be facing your first Christmas without someone you love, and every tradition seems to have an empty space in it. You might be watching someone close to you go through a serious illness, carrying fear and uncertainty about what the new year will bring. You might be going through a separation or divorce, trying to work out the arrangements for Christmas Day while worrying about how your children will cope with a family life that has changed.

For others, it is the family gathering itself that brings a quiet sense of dread. The old tensions, the things that never get said, the pressure to keep everyone happy and the guilt that follows when you cannot. And some people have simply had an exhausting year. They have spent so many months coping that, now Christmas is almost here, there is very little energy left to enjoy it.

You might also be surrounded by people who love you and still feel lonely, because nobody quite understands what you are going through, or because you have not felt able to tell them.

## How it can feel right now

Difficult seasons rarely bring just one emotion. You might recognise some of these.

There may be sadness, because the festivities keep reminding you of someone or something you have lost. There may be anxiety about Christmas Day, about a difficult conversation you know is coming, or about a future that feels uncertain. There is often guilt, for not feeling excited, for wanting some time on your own, or even for enjoying yourself while someone you love is suffering.

You might feel frustrated and resentful, worn down by meeting everyone else's expectations and pretending that everything is fine. If someone you love is unwell, there can be a deep sense of fear and helplessness. And underneath all of it, there is often plain exhaustion.

One thing that surprises many people is that you can look forward to some parts of Christmas and dread others, all at the same time. Joy and grief can sit side by side. Feeling happy does not mean you have forgotten someone, and feeling sad does not mean you are ungrateful for what you have. Both can be true, and both deserve some room.

## The pressure to feel a certain way

What often makes this season so hard is the feeling that Christmas should be experienced in a particular way. You may feel expected to be cheerful, available, organised, grateful and strong. You may feel responsible for protecting everyone else's feelings, especially your children's or those of other family members, so you hold your own feelings back until everyone else is taken care of.

When you are busy simply trying to get through Christmas, there is rarely time to stop and ask yourself the questions that might actually help. What do I need this year? What would genuinely help me? What can I realistically manage? What might I do a little differently?

These are gentle questions, and they can change a great deal. They are often the beginning of making a hard Christmas a little kinder on yourself.

## A few gentle things you can try

You do not need to wait for a session to begin looking after yourself. Here are a few small things that may help in the weeks ahead.

Give yourself permission to do Christmas differently this year. Some traditions may feel comforting to keep, and others may feel too painful right now. You are allowed to choose, and you are allowed to change your mind.

If you are grieving, you might think about a small, quiet way to remember the person you are missing. Lighting a candle, cooking their favourite dish, or simply saying their name out loud at the table can bring them gently into the day.

Try to notice when you are saying yes out of habit or obligation. Before agreeing to the next plan or the next visit, take a breath and ask yourself whether you have the energy for it. A kind "let me think about it" gives you space to decide.

Find at least one person you can be honest with. You do not need to explain everything to everyone, but having one person who knows how you are really feeling can lighten the load a great deal.

And when it all feels like too much, try this short Mindful Moment. Wherever you are, put both feet flat on the floor and notice them there. Take three slow breaths, letting each out a little longer than the one before. Let your shoulders drop. It will not change what is happening, but it can give you a moment of steadiness in the middle of it.

## What happens in the hour

Your Space is a 60-minute, one-to-one coaching session held online. There is no programme to sign up to and no ongoing commitment. You can book it simply because you need some support with something that is happening right now.

The hour is shaped entirely around you. It is a chance to talk openly about what is really going on, without being judged, interrupted, or having to worry about how anyone else will react. Together, we can make sense of the thoughts and feelings you are carrying, and gently work out what you need.

We might look at your expectations for Christmas, the boundaries you would like to set, a difficult decision you are facing, or what is and is not within your control. Where it feels right, I may guide you through a short mindfulness or breathing practice to help you slow down and feel more grounded.

Most people leave with one or two small, realistic steps, a fresh way of looking at things, or a little more confidence about how they want to approach the weeks ahead. Someone who is grieving might explore how they would like to remember their loved one, and which traditions they want to keep or change. Someone caring for a person who is unwell might recognise that they need more practical help, permission to rest, or a way to tell others what they need. Someone going through a separation might think about how to approach Christmas Day with more realistic expectations and a lot more kindness towards themselves.

My hope for the hour is a simple one: to help make a difficult Christmas a little more manageable, a little more meaningful, and a little gentler on you.
