The feeling of having gone missing from your own life rarely means something has gone wrong. More often it means an identity that served you well for twenty five years has quietly stopped fitting.

## The short answer

If you have typed this question into a search bar late at night, here is the most likely explanation. You are in an identity transition. The version of you that has carried the last two or three decades - the competent one, the one who delivers, the one everyone leans on - was built for a set of conditions that have changed. She worked. She may have worked brilliantly. And she is now running on an operating system you have outgrown.

That is a different thing from decline, and a different thing from something being broken in you. Identity transitions are a recognised feature of adult life. They tend to arrive when the external markers of a life are still intact and the internal experience of it has shifted. Herminia Ibarra's book Working Identity is worth reading if you want a serious treatment of how professional identity actually changes.

The disorientation is real. The usual mistake is to interpret it as a personal failing when it is closer to a signal that something structural is asking to be looked at.

## Why it tends to arrive around 50

Fifty is rarely the cause. It is the point at which several things converge and the convergence becomes hard to ignore.

Children become independent or leave. Parents become ill, or die, and you find yourself holding both generations at once. A promotion arrives and produces admiration from everyone except you. Perimenopause changes your sleep, your temperature regulation, your concentration and sometimes your sense of your own mind. A marriage reaches a crossroads. A business you built starts to feel like a job you took.

Any one of these would be significant. Arriving together, they strip away the scaffolding that held your sense of self in place. For many senior women, that scaffolding was made almost entirely of roles - the reliable director, the daughter who sorts things out, the mother who holds the family calendar in her head. When roles thin out or change shape at the same time, what is left underneath can feel unfamiliar.

It is also worth saying plainly that hormonal change can affect mood, memory and confidence, and that is a medical conversation as much as a coaching one. If your symptoms are affecting how you function, speak to your GP.

## What it actually looks like day to day

Women describe this experience in strikingly similar language. It is rarely dramatic. It is usually a slow accumulation of small signals that are easy to explain away individually.

You might recognise some of these:

- Doing your job well and feeling oddly absent while you do it
- A flatness after achievements that would once have genuinely pleased you
- Exhaustion that has little to do with how much sleep you had
- Irritation that surprises you, often directed at people who have done nothing wrong
- Saying yes automatically and resenting it within the hour
- A sense of performing competence rather than inhabiting it
- Nostalgia for a version of yourself you can picture clearly and cannot locate
- Difficulty answering simple questions about what you actually want

## Why framing this as a confidence problem misses the point

The most common advice offered to women in this position is to rebuild confidence. Take on a stretch project. Speak up more. Work on your presence.

For a woman who has run departments, managed partners, carried P&L responsibility and raised children alongside all of it, this advice tends to land badly. Her competence is intact. She can still do the thing. What has changed is her relationship to the thing.

Confidence work asks how you can perform the existing role more convincingly. The question underneath this feeling is different. It asks whether the role still has anything to do with you. Those are not the same enquiry, and answering the first one with more effort usually deepens the tiredness.

There is a second reason the confidence framing fails. It locates the problem entirely inside the woman. A great deal of what produces this experience is environmental: years of over-functioning, the sandwich-generation load, the quiet self-silencing that senior women learn early and rarely unlearn. Those conditions deserve examination in their own right.

## What genuinely helps

The instinct at this stage is often to make a large, decisive move. Resign. Sell the business. End the marriage. Book a sabbatical. Sometimes a significant change is exactly right, and it almost never needs to be made in the first eight weeks of noticing that something is off.

What tends to help first is slower and less dramatic.

Start by separating the strands. The exhaustion, the flatness, the irritation and the sense of absence often have different sources, and treating them as one undifferentiated fog makes the whole thing feel unsolvable. Some of it may be physiological. Some of it is load. Some of it is a genuine mismatch between your values now and the life you built when those values were different.

Then pay attention to energy rather than enthusiasm. Notice which meetings, conversations and tasks leave you with something afterwards and which ones take more than they return. This is slow, unglamorous data, and it is more reliable than any list of your strengths.

Give yourself small moments that belong to nobody else. The first few minutes with a cup of tea before the house wakes. The walk from the car park taken at half speed. The washing-up done with your attention actually on the water. These are moments stolen back, and they matter because they are the only part of the day where you are not being useful to someone.

Finally, resist the pressure to produce an answer. The uncomfortable middle period, where the old identity has loosened and the new one has not arrived, is a real stage rather than evidence of indecision. Chip Conley's Learning to Love Midlife and Arthur C. Brooks's From Strength to Strength are both good company for that stretch.

## A question worth sitting with

You do not need to answer this today. Just notice what arrives when you read it.

Which parts of how I live were chosen by me, and which were simply inherited from a decision I made at thirty and never revisited?

Most women find that the honest answer takes a few days to surface. Let it.
