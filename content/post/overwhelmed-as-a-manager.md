You are the one who notices when someone on your team has gone quiet. You remember whose mum is unwell, who is worried about their review, and who needs a little extra encouragement before a big meeting. You take the difficult news from above and find a way to share it kindly. And then, at the end of a long day, you sit in the car or on the train home and realise that nobody has asked how you are doing. If that feels familiar, I wrote this for you.

## Why being a manager can feel so heavy

Feeling overwhelmed as a manager is far more common than most people admit, and it very rarely means you are bad at your job. More often, it means you are standing in the middle of two kinds of pressure at once. From above come the targets, the restructures and the decisions that were made in rooms you were not in. From below come your team's worries, their workloads and the parts of their lives they bring to work with them. You are expected to keep both steady, usually without any extra time, and often with very little support of your own.

I spent many years in the corporate world before I trained as a coach, and I know how quietly this kind of weight builds up. Most of us are promoted because we were good at the work itself. Very few of us are ever shown how to hold other people's pressure, so we do what caring, conscientious people tend to do. We absorb it.

Someone on your team is anxious about a deadline, so you take on part of their work and say nothing. A colleague is having a hard time at home, so you cover for them without making a fuss. A decision comes down from senior leadership and lands badly, so you soften it, explain it as best you can and quietly take the frustration that follows. Each of those choices comes from a good place. But over weeks and months they add up to something heavy that nobody else can see, and that you may not even fully admit to yourself.

## The loneliness nobody talks about

One of the hardest parts of managing people is how lonely it can feel. You might not feel able to share your doubts with your team, because they look to you for reassurance and you want to give them that. You might not feel able to share them with your own manager either, in case it looks as though you are not coping. So the worrying happens on your own, often late at night when the house is quiet, or at four in the morning when your mind starts going through tomorrow's conversations before you are even properly awake.

It can be a strange feeling to be surrounded by people all day and still feel completely alone with what you are carrying.

## How it can start to affect you

Pressure is part of most jobs worth doing, and a certain amount of it can even feel good. Overwhelm is different. It is what happens when the pressure builds up and has nowhere to go. It often creeps in so gradually that you only notice it when someone at home says, gently, that you seem a bit short with everyone lately.

You might recognise yourself in some of this. You come out of your one-to-ones feeling emptied, as though you have given away the last of your energy. You find yourself doing your team's work for them, because somehow that feels easier than having the conversation. Small requests start to feel enormous, and you snap at things you would once have shrugged off, then feel guilty about it for the rest of the evening. There is a list running in your head that never seems to switch off, even on holiday. You feel responsible for how everyone feels about decisions you had no say in. And you may have stopped asking for help altogether, because you honestly cannot think who you would ask.

If any of that sounds like you, please be kind to yourself about it. What it usually means is that the load you are carrying and the support around you have drifted out of balance, and that is something that can change.

If you are noticing changes in your sleep, your mood or your health, please speak to your GP. Coaching can help with the thinking and the habits that surround work, but it does not replace medical care or therapy.

## Separating what is yours from what you are holding

One question that I find helps managers more than almost anything else is a very simple one: is this mine to fix, or is it mine to support?

So much of what weighs on us at work belongs, in the end, to someone else. When someone on your team is anxious about a presentation, their anxiety is real and you can absolutely support them through it. What you cannot do is make it disappear for them, and trying to do that usually leaves you both more tired. When the board decides on a restructure, your part is to share it honestly and to listen carefully to how it lands with people. You do not have to carry the whole weight of defending every detail of a decision you did not make.

When you begin to sort things in this way, something often softens. You are still caring and still there for your people. You have simply put down the parts that were never yours to carry in the first place.

If you would like to try this, here is a small way to begin. At the end of the working day, write down the three things that are sitting most heavily with you. Next to each one, write either "mine to fix" or "mine to support". Then notice, without judging yourself, how many of them you have been treating as yours to fix when really they were yours to support.

## Small ways to give yourself some breathing space

Many managers tell themselves they will start looking after their own wellbeing once things calm down. The difficulty is that in most organisations, things never quite calm down. So whatever you do for yourself needs to be small enough to fit into the days you already have.

You might start with a short pause between meetings. Before you join the next call, put both feet flat on the floor and take three slow breaths. It takes less than a minute, and it stops you carrying the tension of one conversation straight into the next.

You could protect one hour a week in your diary just for thinking, with no meetings and no messages, and treat it with the same respect you would give a meeting with your own manager.

At the end of the day, try writing tomorrow's first task on a sticky note before you close your laptop. It sounds almost too simple, but it gives your mind permission to stop, because it knows the day has an edge and that nothing will be forgotten.

The next time you feel yourself reaching to take on someone else's work, pause and ask them, "What would help you to do this?" It gives them the chance to grow in confidence, and it gives you back a little of your time.

And when you speak to your own manager, try being honest, plainly and calmly, about what your team can realistically deliver. That honesty is part of your role, and it is also one of the kindest things you can do for the people who work for you.

None of this will transform a demanding job overnight. What these small habits do is create little gaps in your day where you get to be a person as well as a manager.

## Finding somewhere to think out loud

Every manager feels pressure. The ones who cope best with it usually have somewhere to put it. That might be a trusted peer in another team, a mentor outside your line of management, a professional network or a coach.

What matters most is that it is a place where you do not have to keep up the appearance of having everything under control. A place where you can say, "I am honestly not sure I am handling this well," and be met with warmth and curiosity, and without judgement. Coaching can offer exactly that kind of space. It can be especially valuable for managers, because for once the conversation is entirely about you: how you respond when the pressure is on, what you would like to feel differently, and what your own next step might be.

## A question to sit with

You do not need to answer this today. Just notice what comes up for you as you read it.

If you supported yourself the way you support your team, what would you do differently this week?
