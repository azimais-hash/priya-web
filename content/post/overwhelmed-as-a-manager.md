You check in on everyone. You notice who is struggling, you absorb the change that has come down from above, and you translate it into something your team can live with. Then you get home and realise nobody asked how you were. If that sounds familiar, this is for you.

## The short answer

Feeling overwhelmed as a manager is very common, and it rarely means you are bad at the job. More often it means you are sitting in the middle of two sets of pressure. From above come targets, restructures and decisions you did not make. From below come your team's worries, workloads and lives. You are expected to steady both, usually with no more time, and often with very little support of your own.

What tends to help is a clearer sense of what is genuinely yours to carry, a few small habits that give your mind somewhere to rest during the day, and at least one place where you are allowed to think out loud. Those three things, practised gently over a few weeks, tend to make the role feel lighter, even when the workload itself has not changed much.

## Why managers carry so much

Most people are promoted into management because they were good at their work. Very few are taught how to hold other people's pressure. So you do what conscientious people do. You absorb it.

A team member is anxious about a deadline, so you quietly take part of the work. Someone is going through a hard time at home, so you cover for them and say nothing. A decision from senior leadership lands badly, so you soften it, explain it and take the frustration that follows. Each of these is a kind and sensible thing to do. Together, over months, they become a weight that nobody else can see.

There is also the loneliness of the position. You may no longer feel able to share your doubts with your team, because they look to you for reassurance. You may not feel able to share them upwards either, in case it looks as though you are not coping. So the thinking happens alone, often at eleven at night or at four in the morning.

## Signs it has tipped into overwhelm

Pressure in itself is part of most roles worth doing. Overwhelm is when the pressure no longer has anywhere to go. You might notice some of these:

- Your one-to-ones have become the only time you are fully present, and you leave them drained
- You find yourself doing your team's work because it feels quicker than having the conversation
- Small requests feel enormous, and you snap at things you would once have shrugged off
- You keep a running list in your head that never switches off, even on holiday
- You feel responsible for how everyone feels about decisions you did not make
- You have stopped asking for help because you cannot imagine who you would ask

These are signals that the load and the support around it have fallen out of balance, and they say nothing about your ability as a manager.

If you are noticing changes in your sleep, your mood or your health, please speak to your GP. Coaching can help with the thinking and the habits around work, and it does not replace medical care or therapy.

## Separate what is yours from what you are holding

One of the most useful questions a manager can ask is simple: is this mine to fix, or mine to support?

Much of what weighs on managers belongs, in the end, to someone else. Your team member's anxiety about a presentation is real, and you can support them through it. You cannot make the anxiety go away, and trying to do so tends to leave both of you more tired. With a restructure decided by the board, your part is to communicate it honestly and to listen to how it lands. Defending every detail of it belongs elsewhere.

When you start sorting things in this way, something often eases. You are still caring, still available, still involved. You have simply stopped carrying the parts that were never yours to lift.

A small way to practise this: at the end of the day, write down the three things that are sitting heaviest with you. Next to each one, write "mine to fix" or "mine to support". Then notice which ones you have been treating as the first when they belong to the second.

## Small habits that give your mind somewhere to rest

Managers often tell themselves they will look after their own wellbeing once things calm down. In most organisations, things do not calm down. So the habits need to be small enough to fit into the day you already have.

- **A pause between meetings.** Before you join the next call, put both feet on the floor and take three slow breaths. It takes under a minute, and it stops you carrying the tension of one conversation into the next.
- **One protected block a week.** An hour in your diary for thinking, with no meetings and no messages. Treat it as you would a meeting with your own manager.
- **A closing ritual.** At the end of the working day, write tomorrow's first task on a sticky note and close the laptop. It tells your mind that the day has an edge.
- **Fewer rescues.** The next time you reach to take on a piece of someone's work, pause and ask, "What would help you to do this?" instead. It builds their confidence and protects your time.
- **Honest updates upwards.** Telling your own manager, plainly and calmly, what your team can realistically deliver is part of the job. It is also one of the kindest things you can do for your team.

None of these will transform a demanding role overnight. What they do is create small gaps in the day where you get to be a person as well as a manager.

## Find somewhere to think out loud

Every manager feels pressure. The ones who cope best with it usually have somewhere to put it. That might be a trusted peer in another team, a mentor outside your line, a professional network or a coach.

What matters is that it is a space where you do not have to perform competence. A place where you can say, "I am not sure I am handling this well," and be met with curiosity and without judgement. Coaching offers exactly that kind of space, and it can be particularly useful for managers because the conversation stays focused on you: how you respond under pressure, what you want to change, and what your own next step is.

## A question to sit with

You do not need to answer this today. Just notice what comes up when you read it.

If you supported yourself the way you support your team, what would you do differently this week?
