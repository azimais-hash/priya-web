The quiet exit

The women who step back rarely say so in advance

By their late forties and fifties, many of the most experienced women in an organisation are at a decision point. A role that has stopped fitting. A promotion that no longer appeals. Menopause arriving in the middle of a demanding job. Ageing parents, teenagers, a restructure, a long-held plan that needs rethinking.

Most of that thinking happens privately. The organisation usually hears about it late, in a resignation, a withdrawal from progression, or a quiet decision to stop putting herself forward.

This work gives those women somewhere rigorous and confidential to think while the decision is still open, and gives you a reason for them to stay.

Why it matters now

Menopause reaches the work itself

CIPD's 2023 study of more than 2,000 UK working women aged 40 to 60 found that 67% of those experiencing menopause symptoms said the symptoms had a mostly negative effect at work, and 27% reported a negative impact on career progression.

Most employers have the intent, few have the provision

In the DWP's 2024 employer survey, 64% of employers agreed that workplaces should provide support relating to menopause, while 18% had a menopause policy and 5% reported menopause training.

Caring lands on the same decade

The ONS estimates 1.4 million sandwich carers in the UK, 61% of them women, and 51% aged 45 to 64. Many are carrying senior responsibility at the same time.

Careers now run far longer

Women aged 50 to 64 had a 68.2% employment rate in 2025, the highest in the recent series, and women leave the labour market at an average age of 64.7.

From April 2026, employers with 250 or more employees have been encouraged to publish voluntary action plans alongside gender pay gap reporting, with menopause-related support among the areas covered by government guidance.

What this work supports

  • Retention of experienced women at the point where they are quietly deciding whether to stay.
  • Confidence and authority through menopause, caring years and other demanding transitions.
  • Your leadership pipeline, including women who are ready for more and unsure whether they still want it.
  • Career sustainability across a working life that now runs well into the sixties.
  • Returning to work after maternity leave or another extended absence.
  • Manager capability, through workshops that give teams language for conversations most people avoid.

Energy Leadership Index

How your leaders show up when the pressure is on

The Energy Leadership Index is a research-backed attitudinal assessment, recommended by Forbes and available only through iPEC-certified coaches. It looks at attitudes and perceptions, and shows how they shift between an ordinary day and a stressful one.

Every day, mindset shapes how people lead, collaborate and decide. The ELI makes those patterns visible, so a leader can see what she does under pressure and choose differently.

Read more about the ELI

Why energy matters

  • Beyond personality tests. Unlike static personality assessments, the ELI looks at attitudes and perceptions and shows how they change in different situations.
  • Seven levels of leadership. You see where a leader spends most of her energy, from stressful, draining states to those that inspire creativity and performance.
  • Real business impact. Leaders who draw on constructive ("anabolic") energy see better team engagement, greater innovation and stronger results.
  • Quick online assessment. About 20 minutes answering questions on how she thinks and reacts in everyday situations.
  • Personal debrief. A 60 to 90 minute session with me, a certified Energy Leadership practitioner, to explore the results and identify where the growth sits.
  • Action plan. Together we map the shifts that would make the most difference to how she leads.

Three ways organisations use it

  • Individual insight. For executives and managers who want to raise self-awareness and sharpen their impact.
  • Team experience. Gives a whole team shared language for communication, accountability and trust.
  • 360 degree perspective. Adds feedback from colleagues and stakeholders for a fuller picture of a leader's energy.

Maternity returners

The return to work is a transition like any other

Coming back after maternity leave is a pivotal moment for the woman and for the organisation. Without support, capable women can lose confidence, question their place, and start planning an exit long before they announce one.

I work with returners before and after the return: what she wants this phase of her career to look like, how she holds her professional identity alongside a new one, and how she handles the first demanding months with some steadiness.

Nine sessions is my usual recommendation, four before she returns and four or five afterwards.
