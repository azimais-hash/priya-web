How Much Does Life Coaching Cost? A Straight Answer

Priya Khan · 22 September 2026 · 6 min read

Coaching prices vary widely, and most websites are vague about why. Here is what sets the figure, what you are actually buying, and what this practice charges.

The short answer

There is no single going rate for life coaching in the UK, which is exactly why the question is so hard to research privately. Coaching is an unregulated title, so anyone can use it, and the range runs from a few pounds an hour to figures that sit alongside executive advisory work. The price you are quoted tells you less about quality than about the shape of what is being offered.

What is more useful is knowing what moves the number. Credentials, session length, how often you meet, how long the engagement runs, whether assessments are included, whether the work is private or in a group, and whether you are buying a single conversation or a six-month container all change the figure considerably.

For transparency, here are the published prices in this practice. A 30 minute discovery call is free. A single 1:1 clarity coaching session is £98. Longer work, including 1:1 coaching programmes, group programmes such as Rooted & Rising, and corporate workshops, is quoted according to scope and discussed directly, because the right structure depends on what you are actually carrying.

What shapes the price

When you are comparing two coaches whose fees look very different, these are usually the variables doing the work. It is worth reading any coaching page with this list in your hand.

  • Accreditation and training. Whether the coach holds credentials from a recognised body such as the International Coaching Federation, and the level of that credential, affects both fee and the standards they are held to.
  • Specialism. A coach who works with one narrow population, and has done for years, is priced differently from a generalist.
  • Session length and frequency. An hour a month and a 90 minute session every fortnight are different pieces of work.
  • Length of engagement. Single sessions are usually priced per session. Programmes are priced as a whole, because the value sits in the arc rather than in any one conversation.
  • What is included beyond the calls. Assessments, written reflections, preparation, access between sessions, and any materials all sit inside the fee.
  • Private or group. Group work shares the coach’s attention and is priced accordingly.
  • Individual or organisational. Corporate workshops are scoped and priced differently again, because the client is an employer with its own objectives.

What you are actually paying for

It helps to be precise about what the money buys, because the hour in the diary is the smallest part of it.

You are paying for undivided, trained attention on something you have probably been carrying alone. You are paying for someone who holds the thread between sessions, who remembers what you said in week three and notices when week nine contradicts it. You are paying for structure, so that a private reckoning becomes something you can work with rather than something you circle at two in the morning.

You are also paying for a particular kind of relationship. A good coach is a thinking partner. She does not hand you a plan, and she does not applaud from the sidelines. She asks the question you have been avoiding, holds the silence afterwards, and keeps you honest about what you actually want. For many women who are used to being the capable one, that hour is the only place in the week where nobody needs anything from her.

Confidentiality is part of the value too. High-discernment buyers rarely want to be seen working on this. A private container has a cost, and for most senior women it is the whole point.

Why very cheap coaching is worth a second look

Low fees are not automatically a warning sign. Coaches in training, coaches building hours towards a credential, and coaches working in a peer or community setting often charge little and do good work. If your budget is tight, that route is legitimate and worth exploring.

What is worth checking is what sits behind the price. Is the coach accredited, or working towards it under supervision? Is there a clear structure, or an open-ended arrangement that could drift? Is there a contract that sets out confidentiality and cancellation terms? Do they say plainly what is outside their scope?

At the other end, a high fee is not proof of rigour either. The questions are the same in both directions. Ask what the engagement includes, how progress is reviewed, what happens if it is not working, and what the coach will decline to work on.

One session or a programme

The choice between a single session and a longer container is usually a question of what you are trying to do.

A single clarity session suits a decision with edges. You have an offer to accept or decline, a conversation to prepare for, a fortnight of circling the same problem and no clear read on it. One well-structured hour can be enough to see it properly. That is what the £98 session is for.

A programme suits an identity transition, which behaves differently. When the sentence in your head is closer to “I have outgrown my life” than “I need to decide about this job”, a single conversation tends to open something rather than resolve it. The Reclamation Programme™ exists for that: 12 sessions over six months, bi-weekly, moving through five phases of Recognise, Release, Reclaim, Redesign and Rise. The bi-weekly rhythm is deliberate, because time-poor women need space between sessions for the thinking to settle into ordinary life.

A question worth sitting with before you spend anything. You do not need to answer it today. If you imagine the fee already paid, what would you want to have looked at honestly by the end?

Coaching and therapy are priced and scoped differently

This matters, because the two are sometimes compared on price alone. Coaching is forward-facing work with a capable adult on clarity, energy, boundaries and the design of a next phase. It is not treatment, and it does not treat, cure or replace care for depression, anxiety disorders, trauma, or the physical symptoms of perimenopause.

If what you are living with is closer to clinical distress, therapy or medical care is the right spend, and a good coach will say so early rather than take your money. Many women do both, at different times or side by side. That is a sensible use of resources.

Practically, therapy in the UK may be available through the NHS at no cost, which changes the comparison entirely. Private therapy and private coaching are both paid-for, and the difference sits in what each is for rather than in the number.

Working out what is reasonable for you

Rather than hunting for an average, it is more useful to ask what you can commit to without strain, over the length of the engagement, and what you would need to see to consider it well spent. Coaching asks for attention as well as money. If the fee is causing quiet anxiety every month, that tends to sit in the room.

It also helps to be honest about the alternative. Not in a pressuring way. Simply notice what the current arrangement is costing in energy, sleep, patience with the people you love, and the sense of being slightly absent from your own life. That is information, and you are allowed to weigh it.

If you want to test the fit before spending anything, the discovery call is 30 minutes and free. You can bring the question you have not said out loud yet, ask what the work would involve, and leave without booking a thing.

Coaching is not therapy or medical care. If your mood, anxiety or distress feels unmanageable, please speak to your GP, midwife or health visitor. In a crisis, call 999 or go to A&E.
