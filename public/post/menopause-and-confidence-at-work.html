Menopause and Confidence at Work: A Steadier View

Priya Khan · 30 October 2026 · 6 min read

If your competence feels less available to you than it used to, you are not imagining it. Here is a steadier way to understand what is happening, and what tends to help.

The short answer

What most women are describing when they search this is not a collapse in ability. It is a change in how easily ability arrives on demand. Disrupted sleep, a word that will not come when you want it, a wave of heat three minutes before you present to the board. The capability is still there. What has altered is the smoothness of access, and that is a very different thing from losing your edge.

The confidence wobble tends to follow rather than lead. One fumbled sentence in a meeting you would once have walked through without thinking, and the mind files it as evidence. A second instance arrives a fortnight later and gets filed alongside it. Within a few months you have assembled a case against yourself out of ordinary human moments that, in your forties, you would not have noticed at all.

If the physical symptoms themselves are disrupting your sleep, your days or your work, that is a conversation for your GP or a menopause-informed clinician. Coaching is not a substitute for that, and no amount of reframing replaces proper medical assessment.

Why it arrives dressed as a confidence problem

Think about what your professional authority has been built on. For most senior women it is reliability. You are the one who is prepared. The one who holds the detail, reads the room, recovers the meeting when it goes sideways. That reputation took twenty-five years to construct and it rests on a quiet assumption: that your mind and body will do what you ask of them, when you ask.

Perimenopause interrupts that assumption. Not permanently and not comprehensively, but enough. Because your identity at work is bound up with being dependable, an unpredictable body feels like an attack on who you are rather than a phase of life you are passing through.

This is why framing it purely as a confidence issue misses the point. Confidence language sends you looking for affirmations and power poses, when what is actually underway is an identity transition. You are being asked to hold authority on a different basis than the one you have used since your thirties. That is a larger and more interesting piece of work.

The gap between how you feel and how you are seen

Here is something worth knowing. The hesitation you experience as glaring is almost always far quieter from the outside. You hear a three-second pause and register panic. The room hears a senior person taking a moment before answering, which is what senior people do.

The genuine cost is rarely the pause itself. It is the self-monitoring that follows. Running the meeting back afterwards. Scanning faces for evidence that someone noticed. Rehearsing contributions you would once have made off the cuff. That surveillance takes enormous energy, and it is the drain on energy, rather than the symptom, that erodes presence over time.

There is also a real fear underneath, and it deserves naming plainly. Many women at this level are quietly afraid of being seen as diminished, of being written off as past it in a culture that has never been especially kind to women ageing in visible roles. That fear is not irrational. It does, however, make small moments carry weight they cannot bear.

What tends to steady things

None of this is a programme to complete. It is a set of adjustments that senior women often find reduce the friction, chosen from a position of authority rather than apology.

  • Treat your sharpest hours as a resource to be protected. If that is the first ninety minutes of the day, defend them for the thinking that genuinely requires you, and move the reactive work elsewhere.
  • Reduce your reliance on live recall where you reasonably can. Three written lines before a meeting is not a crutch. Plenty of chief executives have never walked into a room without them.
  • Say the thing once and let it sit. Over-explaining is usually a response to feeling less sure, and it reads to a room as less certainty rather than more.
  • Stop the post-meeting audit. When you notice yourself replaying, name it as replaying and return your attention to the next task.
  • Get the physical picture assessed properly. Sleep, symptom management and anything else going on medically belong with your GP.
  • Choose two or three people who get the full picture. Isolation does more damage to professional confidence than symptoms do.

What to say at work, and to whom

There is no single correct answer on disclosure, and anyone who tells you otherwise is not accounting for your organisation, your sector or your position within it. Some women find naming it openly is a relief and a contribution. Others judge, reasonably, that their particular environment would use the information badly.

A middle path that often works at senior level is to speak in terms of what you need rather than what you have. Moving a standing meeting from eight in the morning to ten. Asking for papers twenty-four hours ahead. Taking the window seat in the overheated meeting room. These are requests you have made for operational reasons for years, and you are entitled to make them now.

If you do decide to speak about it directly, notice the difference between explaining yourself and informing people. You are a senior woman passing through an ordinary life stage. That can be stated with the same calm you would bring to any other piece of information about how you work.

A question worth sitting with

Here is a Mindful Moment that costs nothing and asks nothing. Before your next significant meeting, while you are still in the corridor or at your desk, put both feet flat on the floor and feel them there for the length of three breaths. Let the jaw loosen. That is the whole practice. A moment stolen back before you walk into the room.

Then a question. You do not need to answer it, and you certainly do not need to answer it today. Just notice what arises.

If your authority no longer rests on being endlessly reliable, what might it rest on instead?

When it turns out to be larger than work

For a good number of women, the confidence question at work is the visible edge of something broader. The promotion that no longer excites. The sense of having outgrown an operating system that served you well for two decades. A fulfilled life on paper, with a flatness inside it that you have not said aloud to anyone.
