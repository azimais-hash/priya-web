Tired of Being the Capable One: What It Really Costs

Priya Khan · 29 September 2026 · 6 min read

Being the person everyone relies on is a position you were promoted into long before anyone gave you a title. This is a look at what it costs, and what begins to loosen it.

The short answer, before anything else

If you typed that phrase into a search bar late one evening, you are probably not short of sleep in the way a fortnight off would fix. The tiredness sits underneath the work. It is there on a Monday morning after a restorative weekend. It is there on holiday, quietly, three days in.

That is because being the capable one is a role, and roles require maintenance. Every day you perform a set of small, almost invisible acts to keep it intact. You take the thing nobody has picked up. You smooth the meeting before it gets awkward. You answer the message at 10pm because it will be quicker than explaining why you cannot. You absorb the shortfall so that no one has to see there was one.

Each of those acts is minor. Together, sustained across two or three decades, they are a full second job. The fatigue you are feeling is the running cost of that job. It is worth saying plainly: this is not a sign that you have become less resilient or less good at what you do. It is the entirely predictable arithmetic of over-functioning.

How competence quietly became a job description

Very few women arrive at this by accident. It tends to begin as something genuinely rewarding. You were good at holding things together, so you were given more to hold. That earned you trust, visibility and progression. The system worked, and it worked well enough for long enough that it stopped being a choice and started being an identity.

There is usually an early chapter to it too. Perhaps you were the eldest, or the reliable one at home, or the girl who learned that the fastest route to safety was to be no trouble at all. By the time you reached a senior role, the wiring was already in place. Seniority simply gave it more scope.

The difficulty with competence as an identity is that it is self-reinforcing. The more capable you appear, the less anyone thinks to check. Colleagues stop asking whether you have capacity, because you have never once said you did not. Family members stop noticing the logistics, because the logistics always somehow happen. Your reward for carrying the load well is a heavier load, delivered with genuine appreciation.

And there is a particular strain that attaches to senior women in this position. You are often the only one, or one of very few, in the room. Visibility raises the cost of any visible wobble. So you carry it privately, which is the most expensive way to carry anything.

What it actually costs

When women describe this to me, the costs tend to cluster in the same few places. See which of these you recognise, without judging any of them.

  • A steady low-grade resentment that you dislike in yourself, aimed at people who have done nothing obviously wrong
  • Decision fatigue at home that has nothing to do with the size of the decisions
  • A thinning of your own interests, until you would struggle to say what you actually enjoy
  • The habit of pre-empting other people’s needs before you have registered your own
  • Relief, rather than pleasure, when something goes well
  • A private fear that if you stopped performing this role, the respect would go with it
  • Physical tightness that arrives before the thought does, usually in the jaw, shoulders or stomach

Why the usual advice does not touch it

The standard prescriptions are delegate more, set better boundaries, practise saying no. All sensible. Most women reading this have already tried them, often with a good deal of discipline, and found the effect temporary.

The reason is that the behaviour is downstream of something else. Saying no is a technique. Tolerating what happens inside you after you have said it is the actual work. If your nervous system has spent forty years learning that being useful is how you stay safe and valued, then the moment you decline something, you will feel a surge of discomfort that has very little to do with the request itself. Most of us resolve that discomfort by taking the task back.

Boundaries hold when the identity underneath them has shifted. Until then, they leak. This is why the question is rarely how do I do less, and is far more usefully who am I when I am not required. That is a slower question. It is also the one that actually moves things.

It is worth naming the timing too. Many women meet this reckoning in their late forties and fifties, when a milestone birthday, a parent’s illness, an empty house, promotion that fails to excite, or the shifting ground of perimenopause interrupts the autopilot long enough for the question to get through. That convergence is not a decline. It is an old operating system reaching the end of its usefulness.

What begins to loosen it

Change here starts with noticing rather than overhauling. You do not need to dismantle your life, and you almost certainly do not want to.

Start by watching where you volunteer. For one week, simply notice the moments you step in before anyone has asked. Do not change them. Count them. Most women are startled by the number, and that surprise does more than any resolution.

Then pay attention to the pause. Between a request and your yes, there is a gap, often less than a second wide. Widening that gap by even a breath is the single most useful structural change available to you, because it is where choice lives. You may still say yes. Saying it deliberately is a different act from saying it reflexively.

Notice, too, what happens in your body when something is handed to you. The tightening arrives before the reasoning. It is good information, and it is usually faster than your analysis.

And allow that some things will be done less well than you would do them. This is the real threshold. Capability has a shadow side that looks a great deal like control, and the discomfort of watching someone else handle it imperfectly is the price of putting the load down. It is generally worth paying.

If you want reading that sits alongside this, Herminia Ibarra’s Working Identity is clear-eyed about how identity actually changes, and Arthur C. Brooks’s From Strength to Strength is thoughtful company for anyone reconsidering what their strengths are for in the second half of a remarkable life.

A question worth sitting with

You do not need to answer this one. Just notice what arises, and notice how quickly you want to move past it.

If you were no longer the person who holds it all together, what would you be afraid people would discover?

Sit with that for a few days rather than a few minutes. The first answer is usually the socially acceptable one. The second is more interesting.

Alongside it, a Mindful Moment. When you next make tea or coffee, stay with it. Hands around the mug, the weight of it, the heat, the sound of the kitchen. Not a practice to add to the list. A moment stolen back from a day that was already taking them from you.

Where this goes next

Being tired of being the capable one is not a problem of time management or willpower. It is a signal that a way of operating which served you brilliantly has quietly stopped fitting, and that the second half of your life is asking to be designed on different terms.
